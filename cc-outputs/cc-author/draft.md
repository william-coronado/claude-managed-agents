# The Great AI Traffic Split: Why Smart Companies Are Routing Between Open and Closed Models

For the past three years, the enterprise AI conversation has been dominated by a single question: which model should we bet on? OpenAI or Anthropic? Google or Meta? Closed frontier intelligence or open weights you can run yourself?

In 2026, the smartest organizations have quietly stopped answering that question — because they've realized it's the wrong one. Instead of picking a model, they're building **routing layers**: infrastructure that looks at every single request an AI agent makes and decides, in milliseconds, which model should handle it. A frontier model like Claude Opus or GPT-5.5 for the hard reasoning. A cheap, fast open-weight model like Qwen or Llama for the routine, high-volume grunt work. The result is a hybrid architecture that is quickly becoming the default way enterprises deploy agentic AI.

## The Agentic Paradox

The trigger for this shift is what might be called the "agentic paradox." An AI agent that plans, uses tools, and executes multi-step tasks doesn't make one call to a model — it makes dozens, sometimes hundreds. Anthropic's own engineering team, describing the architecture behind Claude's Research feature, found that <cite index="32-1">agents typically use about 4× more tokens than chat interactions, and multi-agent systems use about 15× more tokens than chats.</cite> That multiplier compounds fast: <cite index="35-4">a subagent that recursively spawns more subagents, or a tool that returns oversized results, can multiply a single query's cost by another 10x or more.</cite>

Paying frontier prices for every one of those steps is economically unworkable at scale. But the alternative — routing everything to a cheaper model — risks quality collapse on the handful of steps that actually require deep reasoning. Hybrid routing is the industry's answer: don't choose one model for the whole workflow, choose the *right* model for each step within it.

Anthropic's own research illustrates the pattern in miniature. Their Research feature uses <cite index="32-2">a multi-agent architecture with an orchestrator-worker pattern, where a lead agent coordinates the process while delegating to specialized subagents</cite>. On an internal evaluation, <cite index="31-2">this setup beat single-agent Claude Opus 4 by 90.2%</cite> — not because the underlying model got smarter, but because the architecture let the system spend tokens where they mattered. Notably, <cite index="32-5">token usage by itself explains 80% of the variance, with the number of tool calls and the model choice as the two other explanatory factors</cite> in performance. That's the core insight driving hybrid routing: intelligent allocation of compute — not a single "best" model — is what determines whether an agentic system succeeds.

## Why Route at All: The Three Business Drivers

### 1. Cost — the spreads are enormous

The price gap between frontier closed models and capable open-weight alternatives has widened, not narrowed. One 2026 engineering guide puts it bluntly: <cite index="4-3">the gap between the cheapest usable model and the most capable one runs to roughly 100×, from DeepSeek V4 at around $0.44 per million input tokens to GPT-5.5-pro at $30 input / $180 output.</cite> Together AI, which specializes in hosting open-weight models at scale, says its <cite index="57-4">customers report cost savings of 6x to 60x compared to closed-model pricing for equal or better performance</cite> — with one customer, Decagon, cutting inference costs sixfold after switching.

Academic research backs up the magnitude of the opportunity. RouteLLM, a framework from UC Berkeley, Anyscale, and Canva published at ICLR 2025, showed that <cite index="8-1">cost reductions of over 85% on MT Bench, 45% on MMLU, and 35% on GSM8K as compared to using only GPT-4, while still achieving 95% of GPT-4's performance</cite> were achievable simply by routing simpler queries to a cheaper model and reserving the expensive one for harder queries. The best-performing router needed the frontier model for <cite index="6-1">95% of GPT-4 performance at 14% of GPT-4 calls</cite> — meaning 86% of traffic could be safely offloaded to a fraction of the cost.

### 2. Data sovereignty and IP protection

Cost isn't the only driver. For regulated industries and companies wary of sending proprietary data to a third-party API, open-weight models deployed on owned infrastructure offer control that no closed API can match. As Red Hat's developer team observed, <cite index="45-1">open models are also used in the enterprise... especially in highly regulated sectors (like telecommunications or banking) that have a strict requirement for on-premise deployment and data sovereignty. For example, due to data residency regulations, the usage of AI needs to stay local, so open models are a requirement.</cite>

### 3. Reliability and failover

A routing layer also functions as insurance. If one provider has an outage or gets rate-limited, requests can be silently redirected to an equivalent model elsewhere. As one industry guide notes, <cite index="4-4">routing is also resilience. When the primary provider is rate-limited or down, a routing layer redirects to an equivalent model on another provider automatically — turning a hard outage into a soft, slightly costlier degradation.</cite> Atlassian has experienced this directly: the company reports <cite index="21-2">significant uptime improvements and resilience to incidents—multiple incidents successfully mitigated due to our central detectors and automated fallback.</cite>

## How the Routing Actually Works

Underneath the marketing, hybrid routing systems rely on a handful of recurring technical patterns.

**Orchestrator/executor splits.** The most common agentic pattern pairs a closed frontier model for planning with open-weight models for high-volume execution. The lead model decomposes a task, decides what needs to happen, and hands off well-scoped subtasks to cheaper models that do the repetitive work. Anthropic's guidance to engineering teams captures the logic directly: <cite index="34-4">Choose the right model for the job... The key is balancing three factors: capabilities, speed, and cost... if you're building multi-agent coding frameworks or doing complex financial analysis, you'll want the most capable model available. But if you're processing thousands of straightforward customer support tickets or extracting data from forms, a lighter, faster model will get the job done just as well</cite>.

**Learned routers (cascade routing).** Rather than hand-written rules, frameworks like RouteLLM train a classifier on human preference data to predict whether a "weak" model can handle a given query as well as a "strong" one. The router <cite index="7-1">high-quality responses at a high cost (e.g., GPT-4), and (2) weak models, which offer lower-quality responses at a reduced cost (e.g., Mixtral-8x7B). The objective is to minimize costs while achieving a specific performance target, e.g., 90% of the strong model, by intelligently routing simpler queries to a weak model and reserving more complex queries for the strong model.</cite> Crucially, these routers generalize: researchers found <cite index="9-1">our routers exhibit strong generalization capabilities, maintaining performance even when routing between LLMs not included in training</cite>, meaning a router trained on one model pair keeps working even after providers ship new model versions.

**Semantic and complexity-based routing.** Newer systems, like the vLLM Semantic Router, classify queries by domain and difficulty before dispatch, sometimes deciding whether a query even needs extended "reasoning" mode at all — since chain-of-thought reasoning can be <cite index="6-4">150× more energy than standard inference, according to research cited in the arXiv paper "When to Reason: Semantic Router for vLLM"</cite>, and models can "overthink" simple queries or "underthink" complex ones.

**Credit-weighted routing in production.** Atlassian's Rovo Dev illustrates this in a live product: it recently introduced <cite index="22-1">model-based credit multipliers</cite> where <cite index="22-2">a task that requires 50 credits with a 1.0x multiplier would consume only 20 credits with a 0.4x multiplier for a smaller model... This way, you can select less complex models for everyday tasks like documentation, and more advanced ones for complex code generation.</cite>

## The Numbers Behind the Trend

Hybrid routing isn't a niche engineering trick — it's becoming standard operating procedure. Enterprise generative AI spend has exploded from <cite index="42-5">$1.7B to $37B since 2023, now capturing 6% of the global SaaS market</cite>, and companies spent <cite index="42-5">$37 billion on generative AI in 2025, up from $11.5 billion in 2024, a 3.2x year-over-year increase</cite>. Enterprises are increasingly running multiple models side by side rather than standardizing on one: survey data shows <cite index="43-4">37% of enterprises using 5+ models in production environments</cite>, reflecting the recognition that <cite index="43-4">different models excel at different tasks—GPT-4 for complex reasoning, Claude for nuanced understanding, and specialized models for domain-specific applications</cite>.

Open-weight models' share of that mix is real but has been volatile. Menlo Ventures' enterprise survey found open-source model usage at <cite index="42-2">a decline in overall enterprise open-source share from 19% last year to 11% today</cite>, with <cite index="42-2">Llama remain[ing] the most widely adopted open-weight model in the enterprise</cite> despite its stagnation. Enterprises also remain notably cautious about Chinese open-weight models specifically — collectively <cite index="42-3">just 1% of total LLM API usage (roughly 10% of enterprise open-source)</cite> — even as adoption looks very different outside large enterprises: <cite index="42-3">vLLM and OpenRouter... show rapidly rising adoption for Qwen, DeepSeek (V3, R1), Moonshot/Kimi, MiniMax, and Z AI's GLM</cite>. The broader OpenRouter usage data tells a similar dual-track story at the token level, where <cite index="48-4">proprietary RoW models retained the largest share (70% on average)</cite> of weekly token volume even as open alternatives grew.

The infrastructure specialists riding this wave are attracting serious capital. Together AI, which hosts open-weight models at scale, just raised <cite index="57-1">an $800 million Series C financing at an $8.3 billion post-money valuation</cite>, reporting that <cite index="57-4">open source model usage across the industry has tripled in twelve months</cite>. Meanwhile OpenRouter — the universal routing API that sits on top of both open and closed models — has seen its valuation more than double in a year, <cite index="12-1">landing at about $1.3 billion post-money</cite>, up from <cite index="12-2">an estimated $547 million post-money valuation it hit a year ago</cite>. The company now processes <cite index="12-3">over 400 models... 8 million global users and 100 trillion tokens processed per month</cite>, a fivefold jump from six months prior — evidence, as TechCrunch put it, that <cite index="12-3">AI work has shifted from training to inference to, now, agents</cite>, and that OpenRouter's gateway "helps enterprises and other AI users select different models for different jobs to control costs or increase reasoning and accuracy for the task at hand."

## The Vendor Ecosystem

A cottage industry of "AI gateway" companies has emerged to make hybrid routing practical without every engineering team building it from scratch:

- **OpenRouter** provides <cite index="11-2">a universal API that provides developers access to 400+ different large language models from providers like OpenAI, Anthropic, Google, and 60+ other labs through a single endpoint</cite>, monetizing through <cite index="11-4">a usage-based monetization model, taking a 5% commission on all inference spend that flows through its routing layer</cite>.
- **RouteLLM**, the open-source academic framework, functions as a <cite index="1-2">drop-in replacement for OpenAI's client (or launch an OpenAI-compatible server) to route simpler queries to cheaper models</cite>.
- **Martian**, a commercial routing startup, focuses on <cite index="5-3">understanding model internals to predict performance, rather than just analyzing query characteristics</cite>, and <cite index="5-3">received investment from Accenture in 2025</cite>.
- **Portkey** differentiates on <cite index="14-3">observability and DevOps features, positioning itself as the monitoring layer for LLM applications</cite>, while **Not Diamond** <cite index="5-3">uses evaluation data to predictively determine model selection, automatically routing each query to the best-suited LLM</cite>.
- **Together AI** and similar "neoclouds" take a different angle — rather than routing across many providers, they build dedicated infrastructure to make hosting open-weight models cheap and fast at enterprise scale, describing themselves as <cite index="57-3">the AI Native Cloud, combining state-of-the-art open-source models, high-performance infrastructure, and frontier research in AI efficiency and scalability</cite>.

## Hybrid Routing in the Wild

The pattern is already embedded in some of the biggest software platforms in use today.

**Atlassian** built an internal AI Gateway that now offers developers <cite index="21-1">a curated model garden of 20+ LLMs, audio and image models from 5+ providers, and 10+ model families</cite>, powering <cite index="21-5">100+ use cases and experiments built across 8+ Atlassian apps, 40+ Atlassian teams</cite>. Rovo, Atlassian's AI agent product, blends closed frontier models with its own open-weight deployments: it <cite index="29-2">may use a diverse range of open-source, Atlassian-hosted LLMs, including models from the LLama series, Phi series, and Mixtral, alongside third-party hosted LLMs from OpenAI's GPT series of models, Anthropic's Claude series of models, and Google's Gemini series of models, to deliver the best outcomes for customers</cite>. Atlassian's own engineers report that this flexibility let them provision new models almost instantly: <cite index="21-2">we were able to provision and prepare production GPT-5 capacity for our developers the day of the launch—Rovo Dev rolled out GPT-5 to users on launch day.</cite>

**Airbnb and Cursor** exemplify the shift toward open-weight models for specific, well-scoped tasks. According to Menlo Ventures' research, <cite index="42-3">Airbnb, for instance, relies on Qwen heavily for its user-facing AI features, while Cursor uses the model as the open-source base for its internal model.</cite>

**Enterprise infrastructure buyers more broadly** are voting with their wallets: nearly three in four organizations, per McKinsey data cited by Together AI, <cite index="57-4">expect to increase their use of open-source AI</cite>, and industry-wide open-source usage has tripled in the past year according to Together's own bookings data.

## The Risks Nobody Puts on the Slide

Hybrid routing isn't free of trade-offs, and the engineering and governance burden is real.

**Silent quality regression.** Because a router's job is to send traffic to a cheaper model whenever it thinks that's "good enough," mistakes are often invisible until they aren't. As one 2026 engineering guide warns, <cite index="4-2">routing to cheaper models can degrade answers in ways that surface as customer tickets days later, not on dashboards.</cite>

**Cost compounding in multi-agent systems.** The savings math that works cleanly for a single routed request gets much messier across a full agentic pipeline. Analysis of production multi-agent deployments finds that <cite index="39-3">you budget for three agents at three times the single-agent cost, then watch the actual bill come in at five, eight, sometimes fifteen times higher... the same context gets passed around and re-billed, work gets redone whenever something fails, and an orchestrator sits on top of it all, burning tokens just to keep the workflow on track.</cite>

**Failure cascades.** Production teams running Anthropic-style orchestrator/worker architectures have found that the published blueprints leave out crucial failure-handling logic: <cite index="35-4">the blueprint describes the happy path. Production has to handle a subagent that times out, returns malformed output, hits a rate limit, or fails its tool call. The lead agent needs to know whether to retry, fail over, partial-result, or abort the whole run, and that logic is not in the blueprint.</cite>

**Governance shifts, it doesn't disappear.** Choosing open weights over a closed API doesn't remove governance burden — it relocates it in-house. Enterprises adopting on-premise open models still need the observability, guardrails, and platform maturity that closed-API vendors previously handled for them, as Red Hat notes about the tooling required to run models like Granite, Qwen, and Llama responsibly at scale: <cite index="45-4">this provides the observability, guardrails, and more that are needed for enterprise AI deployments.</cite>

## Where This Is Headed

The trajectory is unmistakable. Enterprises are not converging on a single winning model — they're converging on infrastructure that lets them use *many* models intelligently. OpenRouter's growth from processing <cite index="17-2">over $100 million in annualized inference spend</cite> in mid-2025 to becoming a unicorn valued at $1.3 billion within a year, alongside Together AI's leap to an $8.3 billion valuation on the strength of enterprises "ditching closed models for open-source" workloads, are two sides of the same coin: routing infrastructure and open-weight hosting are both becoming essential, unglamorous plumbing for the agentic AI era.

As one OpenRouter investor summary put it, the multi-model future isn't a prediction anymore — <cite index="12-3">companies have no plans to get locked into a model vendor as they did with their various SaaS providers. The multi-model future is already here.</cite> For any organization building serious agentic AI workflows, the strategic question is no longer "which model do we standardize on," but "how do we build a routing layer smart enough to make that question irrelevant."

The winners in this next phase of enterprise AI won't necessarily be the labs with the single smartest model. They'll be the organizations — and the infrastructure vendors serving them — that master the art of sending each piece of work to exactly the model it deserves: frontier intelligence for the moments that require it, and fast, cheap, open-weight compute for everything else.
